# COPYWRITING CONSTITUTION
## Master Copy Manifest — Version 1.0
### Companion standard to `manifest.txt` and `manifestedicao.md`

**Status:** Mandatory operating standard for researching, planning, writing, editing, reviewing, and implementing copy for every website and digital product in this repository.
**Language principle:** Simple, natural, conversational, concrete, sequential, truthful, and specific to the actual business.
**Primary objective:** Help the right visitor understand the offer, recognize its relevance, trust what can be proven, and know what to do next.
**Relationship:** This document governs copy and verbal communication. The master `manifest.txt` governs the full project and execution process. `manifestedicao.md` governs visual and editorial art direction. All three documents must be read and applied together.

# PART I — THE CENTRAL COPY PHILOSOPHY

## 1. THE PURPOSE OF COPY

Copy is not a collection of impressive sentences. It is the language through which a business explains itself and helps a visitor make a decision.

Every meaningful piece of copy should do at least one of these jobs:
- Help the visitor understand what the business is, sells, or does.
- Show who the offer is for and when it is relevant.
- Explain a problem, need, product, service, process, or outcome.
- Make a real difference or advantage understandable.
- Reduce uncertainty with useful detail or evidence.
- Answer a question or objection.
- Help the visitor take the next appropriate action.
- Express the real personality of the organization.

If a sentence does none of these things and exists only to sound sophisticated, emotional, premium, innovative, or persuasive, remove it or rewrite it.

**The standard is not “Does this sound like advertising?” The standard is “Does this help the right person understand and decide?”**

## 2. THE DEFAULT VOICE: HUMAN, CLEAR, AND CONVERSATIONAL

Unless the brand has a well-founded reason to use another voice, write as a knowledgeable person explaining the business to another person.

The default language must be:
- Simple without being simplistic.
- Conversational without becoming careless.
- Professional without sounding institutional or stiff.
- Persuasive without becoming pushy.
- Confident without making unsupported claims.
- Warm when the business relationship calls for warmth.
- Direct when the visitor needs information quickly.
- Natural when read aloud.

Use words people normally use in real conversations. Prefer familiar verbs and concrete nouns. Do not choose a complicated word when an ordinary word communicates the same thing more clearly.

Write in the language and variety appropriate to the actual audience. Do not assume that Portuguese for Angola, Brazil, Portugal, or another market is interchangeable. Respect local vocabulary, spelling, currency, forms of address, idioms, and levels of formality. If the target market is unclear and materially affects the copy, ask.

### 2.1 Conversational does not mean casual everywhere

A bank, law firm, clinic, hotel, restaurant, creative studio, and youth brand should not sound identical. Conversational language is a way of being understandable, not a fixed level of informality.

Choose formality based on:
- Audience expectations.
- Risk and seriousness of the decision.
- Brand personality.
- Industry conventions that genuinely matter.
- Local culture and relationship between business and customer.

Do not use slang, jokes, emojis, or familiar address forms merely to appear human. Use them only when they fit the brand and audience.

## 3. SIMPLE LANGUAGE, NOT EMPTY LANGUAGE

Simple language must still communicate something meaningful.

Prefer:
- Specific descriptions over broad adjectives.
- Active verbs over abstract nouns.
- Short, readable sentences over long, nested sentences.
- Everyday vocabulary over inflated vocabulary.
- Examples over unsupported generalizations.
- Clear explanations over slogans that conceal the offer.

Avoid:
- Unnecessary jargon.
- Corporate filler.
- Academic phrasing in ordinary commercial contexts.
- Forced metaphors.
- Decorative adjectives.
- Repeated claims with slightly different wording.
- Sentences that sound important but say very little.

A short sentence is not automatically clear. A long sentence is not automatically bad. Judge each sentence by whether a real visitor can understand it on the first reading.

## 4. CONVERSATIONAL, NOT POETIC BY DEFAULT

The default website voice is not literary poetry, motivational writing, grand declarations, or cinematic narration.

Do not turn ordinary business facts into phrases such as:
- “Onde os sonhos ganham vida.”
- “Uma nova forma de viver o extraordinário.”
- “Mais do que serviços, criamos experiências.”
- “O futuro começa aqui.”
- “A excelência encontra a inovação.”
- “Transformamos momentos em memórias inesquecíveis.”

These expressions are not automatically forbidden in every possible brand. They are forbidden as lazy defaults. They may only be used when the brand has a real, specific reason for them and the surrounding copy gives them meaning.

Do not use metaphor to hide what the company actually does. If a metaphor is part of the approved brand concept, explain the offer plainly nearby.

**Creativity belongs in the concept, insight, structure, and specificity of the message. It does not require complicated language.**

## 5. SEQUENTIAL COPY: THE WEBSITE IS A CONVERSATION

A website must not feel like unrelated blocks of marketing text. Its message should unfold in a logical sequence.

The visitor should be able to move naturally through these questions:

1. **What is this?** Identify the company, product, service, or offer.
2. **Is it relevant to me?** Show the audience, situation, need, or use case.
3. **How can it help?** Explain the offer and how it works.
4. **Why this option?** Present meaningful differences and reasons to choose it.
5. **Can I trust it?** Show real evidence, process, people, work, or transparent details.
6. **What should I know before deciding?** Answer relevant questions and objections.
7. **What do I do now?** Give a clear next step.

This is a communication logic, not a mandatory seven-section template. The page architecture must follow the product, audience, content, and visual direction.

Each section must prepare the next one. Do not repeat the same promise in the hero, introduction, services, benefits, and final CTA. Advance the conversation.

### 5.1 One section, one main job

Before writing a section, state its job in one sentence. Examples:
- Introduce the offer.
- Explain the service process.
- Help compare available options.
- Show the work.
- Establish trust through verifiable evidence.
- Answer a common concern.
- Guide the visitor to contact the team.

If two sections have the same job, combine them or give each a distinct purpose. If a section has no clear job, remove it.

### 5.2 Use transitions in meaning, not filler phrases

The flow should feel connected without repeatedly using phrases such as “E não é tudo”, “Mas há mais”, “Além disso”, “Por outro lado” or “E sabe o melhor?”.

Create continuity through the order of ideas. The next section should answer a question raised by the previous one.

# PART II — RESEARCH BEFORE WRITING

## 6. NEVER WRITE FROM THE BUSINESS CATEGORY ALONE

Knowing that a business is a restaurant, clinic, construction company, hotel, agency, shop, photographer, or software company is not enough to write its copy.

Before writing, understand the actual business:
- What it sells or provides.
- Who it serves.
- Where and how it operates.
- What customers come to it for.
- How the product or service works.
- What customers value.
- What makes the business meaningfully different.
- What evidence supports its claims.
- What the visitor needs to know before acting.
- What the business wants the website to achieve.

Two businesses in the same sector may need completely different messages, vocabulary, arguments, and tone.

## 7. REQUIRED DISCOVERY INTERVIEW

Conduct an adaptive interview. Ask only what is missing. Do not send a long questionnaire if a short conversation can uncover the same information. Do not ask the user to repeat facts already supplied.

### 7.1 Business identity
- What is the business called?
- How did it start, and why does it exist?
- What does it offer in practical terms?
- Which products or services are most important?
- What locations or markets does it serve?
- What should a new visitor understand first?

### 7.2 Audience
- Who is the main customer?
- What situation makes that person look for this offer?
- What are they trying to achieve?
- What do they worry about?
- What do they compare before choosing?
- What words do customers use when describing the need?

### 7.3 Offer and value
- What exactly does the customer receive?
- What is included and what is not?
- How does the process work?
- What problem does it solve or what need does it meet?
- What makes the offer useful?
- What alternatives does the customer have?
- Why might someone choose this business instead?

### 7.4 Differentiation
- What does the business do differently in practice?
- Is the difference in the product, service, process, speed, location, selection, expertise, convenience, relationship, price, or something else?
- Can the difference be demonstrated?
- What do customers praise most often?
- What can the business confidently promise and consistently deliver?

### 7.5 Proof and trust
- How long has the business operated, if known?
- Which clients, projects, qualifications, licenses, facilities, processes, or results may be mentioned?
- Are testimonials available and authorized?
- Are numbers and performance claims documented?
- What can be shown visually?
- Which claims must not be made?

### 7.6 Objections and decisions
- What questions do customers ask before buying?
- What makes them hesitate?
- What information is often missing?
- What are the concerns about price, quality, timing, delivery, payment, safety, warranty, support, or availability?
- What happens after the visitor contacts the business?

### 7.7 Brand voice
- Should the business sound formal, friendly, direct, technical, warm, playful, discreet, authoritative, or another way?
- How does the team speak to customers in real life?
- Which expressions sound natural to the business?
- Which words or styles should never be used?
- Should the website use “tu”, “você”, “o senhor/a senhora”, or a neutral construction? Decide for the target market and brand.

### 7.8 Website objective
- What is the primary action: call, WhatsApp, request a quote, book, buy, visit, subscribe, download, or something else?
- What secondary actions matter?
- What information must be visible immediately?
- What does a successful visit look like?

If the user cannot answer, help them reason through the options. Do not turn uncertainty into invented facts.

## 8. SOURCE OF TRUTH AND FACTUAL DISCIPLINE

Create a clear distinction between:
- **Confirmed facts:** explicitly supplied by the owner or supported by reliable material.
- **Observed details:** directly visible in supplied assets or verified public information.
- **Interpretations:** reasonable conclusions drawn from the facts.
- **Creative proposals:** possible ways to position or express the business that still need approval.
- **Unknowns:** information that has not been provided or verified.

Never silently convert an interpretation or proposal into a confirmed business fact.

Do not invent:
- Years in business.
- Number of customers, projects, branches, products, or employees.
- Awards, certifications, accreditations, licenses, or partnerships.
- Testimonials, reviews, endorsements, or customer quotes.
- Sales figures, success rates, rankings, guarantees, or performance results.
- Delivery times, availability, prices, discounts, warranties, or service coverage.
- Materials, ingredients, methods, qualifications, or technical specifications.
- A founder's story, mission, values, or personal motivations.

If a useful claim lacks evidence, either remove it, qualify it accurately, or ask for confirmation.

### 8.1 Never fabricate social proof

Do not create fake testimonials, fake customer names, fake logos, fake review counts, fake ratings, or fabricated case studies. A placeholder must be clearly identified as a placeholder during development and must not be presented as genuine in the final website.

### 8.2 Do not promise what the business cannot control

Avoid guarantees and absolute claims unless the business has explicitly authorized them and can honor them. Be precise about conditions, limits, eligibility, availability, and dependencies.

### 8.3 Voice-of-customer evidence

Customer language is valuable only when it comes from a real source.

Useful sources may include:
- Customer interviews supplied by the business.
- Authorized reviews and testimonials.
- Real questions received through WhatsApp, email, calls, or support.
- Sales conversations and objections recorded by the team.
- Search queries or feedback data when legitimately available.

Rules:
- Never invent a customer quote or present an AI-generated sentence as something a customer said.
- Do not claim that a phrase is commonly used by customers unless there is evidence.
- Remove personal information and obtain permission where a quote, name, image, or identifiable story is to be published.
- When no customer-language evidence exists, use plain language based on confirmed facts. Label audience assumptions as hypotheses internally, not as research findings.
- Do not imitate spelling mistakes, dialect, slang, or personal speech to manufacture authenticity.


Avoid guarantees and absolute claims unless the business has explicitly authorized them and can honor them. Be precise about conditions, limits, eligibility, availability, and dependencies.

# PART III — STRATEGY AND PERSUASION

## 9. DEFINE THE MESSAGE BEFORE WRITING THE HEADLINE

Before drafting, define a compact message strategy:

1. **Audience:** who is the message for?
2. **Situation:** what brings this person here?
3. **Need:** what do they want or need?
4. **Offer:** what does the business provide?
5. **Value:** why is it useful?
6. **Difference:** what makes this option meaningfully distinct?
7. **Evidence:** what can support the message?
8. **Objection:** what could prevent action?
9. **Action:** what should the visitor do next?
10. **Voice:** how should this business sound?

Then write the central message in plain language. If the offer cannot be explained clearly in one or two sentences, the strategy is not ready.

## 10. THE CENTRAL PROMISE

A strong promise tells the visitor what the business can actually help them do, obtain, understand, or experience.

A useful promise is:
- Relevant to the target audience.
- Specific enough to understand.
- Connected to the actual offer.
- Credible and supportable.
- Clear about scope and conditions.
- Distinct from generic claims competitors could all make.

Do not confuse a promise with an aspiration. “Ser a melhor empresa do mercado” is an aspiration or unsupported superiority claim, not a useful customer promise.

Prefer a concrete explanation of what the business provides and how it helps.

## 11. BENEFITS, FEATURES, AND MECHANISM

Use the three layers correctly:

- **Feature:** what the product or service has or includes.
- **Practical benefit:** what that feature helps the customer do.
- **Mechanism/process:** how the business delivers that benefit.

Example:
- Feature: online menu with categories and prices.
- Practical benefit: customers can see the available dishes before ordering.
- Mechanism: the restaurant shares one mobile-friendly menu link or QR code.

Do not turn every feature into a dramatic emotional transformation. Some customers simply need accurate information, convenience, availability, or a reliable service.

Explain the mechanism when it makes the offer easier to understand or more credible.

## 12. DIFFERENTIATION MUST BE REAL

“Qualidade”, “excelência”, “inovação”, “profissionalismo”, “compromisso” and “atendimento personalizado” are not meaningful differentiators by themselves. Almost every competitor can say the same thing.

When these words are relevant, explain what they mean in practice.

Ask:
- What does the business do that the customer can notice?
- What is different in the actual process?
- What is included that alternatives may not include?
- What does the business specialize in?
- What does it do particularly well?
- What evidence makes this distinction credible?

If no defensible difference exists, do not manufacture one. Position the offer around the real strengths that can be demonstrated.

## 13. PERSUASION WITHOUT MANIPULATION

Persuasion should help the visitor make a well-informed decision.

Use:
- Clear explanations.
- Relevant benefits.
- Verifiable proof.
- Transparent terms.
- Specific examples.
- Helpful comparisons.
- Answers to real objections.
- A clear next step.

Do not use:
- Fake scarcity.
- Invented deadlines.
- False countdowns.
- Fake popularity or customer counts.
- Guilt, shame, fear, or humiliation.
- Unsupported guarantees.
- Exaggerated claims.
- Hidden conditions.
- Aggressive repetition of CTAs.
- Misleading before-and-after claims.
- Pressure that disguises uncertainty.

Urgency may be used only when a real deadline, limited stock, appointment availability, or operational constraint exists and is accurately represented.

## 14. OBJECTIONS: ANSWER THE REAL QUESTION

Objection handling must be based on actual customer concerns, not a generic sales script.

Common areas may include:
- Price and what is included.
- Quality and materials.
- Delivery, installation, or turnaround.
- Availability and scheduling.
- Payment methods.
- Returns, warranty, or after-sales support.
- Location and service coverage.
- Privacy, security, or data handling.
- Experience and qualifications.
- What happens after contacting the business.

Answer clearly and honestly. If the answer depends on the customer's case, say so and guide them to ask.

Do not invent an FAQ just to fill a page. Include questions that visitors genuinely need answered.

# PART IV — REFERENCE THINKERS AND HOW TO USE THEM

## 15. LEARN PRINCIPLES, DO NOT IMITATE A COPYWRITER

The system may draw on established copywriting and advertising principles. These are intellectual references, not voices to impersonate and not templates to reproduce.

### 15.1 Gary Bencivenga — research, proof, and argument

Learn from:
- Deep research before writing.
- Understanding the product and market.
- Building a logical case.
- Supporting claims with proof.
- Making the argument relevant to the reader.
- Editing until every statement earns its place.

Use this influence when the offer requires careful explanation, credibility, or a strong evidence-based case.

Do not imitate his phrasing, recreate a famous control, or turn every website into long-form direct response.

### 15.2 Alex Hormozi — offer clarity and value

Learn from:
- Making the offer understandable.
- Clarifying the desired outcome.
- Explaining value and what is included.
- Reducing uncertainty and friction.
- Addressing objections.
- Showing why the offer is worth considering.

Use this influence when a business needs to explain packages, services, products, pricing, or a commercial proposition.

Do not import exaggerated promises, aggressive sales language, or a formula that does not fit the business.

### 15.3 David Ogilvy — audience understanding and clear advertising

Learn from:
- Researching the audience.
- Writing headlines that communicate.
- Respecting the reader's intelligence.
- Giving useful information.
- Connecting creative ideas to the product.
- Making claims understandable and credible.

Use this influence for clear positioning, service explanations, product communication, and brand advertising.

Do not reproduce historical advertising conventions that no longer fit the audience or medium.

### 15.4 Eugene Schwartz — awareness and desire

Learn from:
- Understanding what the audience already knows.
- Distinguishing between awareness of a problem, awareness of a solution, and awareness of a specific offer.
- Meeting the visitor at their actual level of understanding.
- Using the language and motivations that already exist in the market.

Use this influence to decide what the hero should explain and how much context the page needs.

Do not assume every visitor is highly aware, ready to buy, or motivated by the same desire.

### 15.5 The combined operating model

Use the references as complementary lenses:
- Bencivenga: Is the argument researched and supported?
- Hormozi: Is the offer and its value clear?
- Ogilvy: Is the message relevant and easy to understand?
- Schwartz: Does the message meet the audience's level of awareness?

The final voice must belong to the business, not to any of these writers.

Do not mention these names in customer-facing copy unless the website is specifically about them or their work.

# PART V — WRITING THE WEBSITE

## 16. THE HERO: ANSWER THE FIRST QUESTION

The hero is not a place to display the most poetic sentence. It is where the visitor should quickly understand the page's subject and relevance.

A strong hero usually contains:
- A clear headline.
- Supporting text that explains or adds useful context.
- One primary call to action.
- An optional secondary action when it has a real purpose.
- Relevant visual material that supports the message.

The hero must answer, as quickly as the project requires:
- What is being offered?
- Who is it for or why should I care?
- What can I do next?

Not every hero needs to answer all three in the same sentence. The total composition must make them clear.

### 16.1 Headline rules

- Make the subject and value understandable.
- Use natural sentence structure.
- Prefer a specific idea over a generic slogan.
- Avoid vague claims such as “A solução que procura”, “O futuro começa aqui” or “A diferença está em nós”.
- Do not force a pun, metaphor, rhyme, or wordplay.
- Do not write a headline that could fit thousands of unrelated businesses.
- Do not make a claim the business cannot support.
- Keep the line length appropriate to the visual direction.
- Do not distort the meaning merely to create a dramatic line break.

A creative headline is welcome when it remains understandable and is supported by clear explanatory copy.

### 16.2 Supporting text

Use supporting text to clarify what the headline cannot explain alone:
- The actual service or product.
- The target customer or use case.
- A key detail about the offer.
- The way to proceed.

Avoid repeating the headline in different words. Usually one or two concise sentences are enough, but the real requirement is usefulness and readability, not a fixed word count.

## 17. SECTION-BY-SECTION COPY

Each section must have a clear communication role. Possible roles include:

### Introduction / About
Explain who the business is and what matters about it. Avoid empty declarations about passion, excellence, or being a market leader. Include origin or history only when confirmed and relevant.

### Services
Name each service in terms the audience understands. Explain what it includes, who it helps, and what the customer can expect. Do not rely on clever service names without plain explanations.

### Products
Use accurate names, descriptions, specifications, prices, options, availability, and conditions. Never invent product attributes or benefits.

### Process
Explain the steps the customer will actually follow. Use the real process, not a fictional idealized workflow.

### Benefits
Connect features to practical value. Avoid repeating the same benefit across multiple cards or sections.

### Differentiation
Explain specific, defensible distinctions. Show the process or evidence where possible.

### Portfolio / Projects
Describe real work accurately. Provide context, the challenge, the work performed, and the outcome only when the information is available. Do not invent results.

### Testimonials / Reviews
Use genuine, authorized material. Preserve the meaning of the original statement. Do not rewrite a testimonial into a stronger claim without approval.

### Pricing / Packages
State what is included, what is optional, and what conditions apply. If pricing is unavailable, explain how to request it. Do not create fake “starting at” prices.

### FAQ
Answer actual questions in plain language. Do not use the FAQ to repeat marketing claims.

### Contact
Explain how to get in touch, what happens after contact, and any useful information needed to prepare.

### Final CTA
Restate the next useful action in a direct way. Do not introduce a new promise or a new argument at the last moment.

## 18. CALLS TO ACTION (CTA)

A CTA should tell the visitor what happens when they click.

Prefer action labels such as:
- Pedir orçamento.
- Ver o menu.
- Consultar disponibilidade.
- Marcar uma consulta.
- Conhecer os serviços.
- Ver os projetos.
- Falar com a equipa.
- Comprar agora.
- Solicitar informações.

Use the actual action supported by the website. If the button opens WhatsApp, make the label and surrounding context clear. If it starts a booking process, do not label it as a generic “Saiba mais”.

Avoid vague CTAs such as “Descubra o extraordinário”, “Comece a sua jornada” or “Eleve o seu potencial” unless they are genuinely appropriate and the action is obvious.

### 18.1 CTA hierarchy

- Define one primary action for each page or key journey.
- Use secondary actions only when they help a different visitor need.
- Do not make every button visually dominant.
- Repeat a CTA when the page length and visitor journey justify it, not automatically after every section.
- Keep the destination and behavior truthful.

## 19. MICROCOPY AND INTERFACE LANGUAGE

Copy includes more than headlines and paragraphs. It also includes:
- Navigation labels.
- Button text.
- Form labels.
- Input placeholders.
- Validation messages.
- Empty states.
- Loading messages.
- Success and error messages.
- Product labels.
- Filters and sorting.
- Confirmation messages.
- Privacy and consent text.

Write these elements to help people complete a task.

Rules:
- Use clear action verbs.
- Tell users what information is needed and why when relevant.
- Do not use placeholder text as a replacement for a proper label.
- Make errors explain what happened and how to fix it.
- Make success messages confirm what actually happened.
- Do not claim that a message was sent, an order was placed, or data was saved unless it was.
- Avoid jokes in errors or sensitive moments.
- Keep labels consistent across the whole product.

# PART VI — HUMAN VOICE AND ANTI-AI EDITING

## 20. WHAT MAKES COPY FEEL MACHINE-GENERATED

Copy often feels artificial when it:
- Uses broad claims without concrete information.
- Repeats the same promise in every section.
- Starts every paragraph with a grand statement.
- Uses symmetrical lists and repeated three-part constructions mechanically.
- Overuses words such as “inovador”, “excelência”, “transformador”, “único”, “extraordinário”, “personalizado”, “solução”, and “experiência”.
- Uses a metaphor in every heading.
- Treats every business as if it were changing lives.
- Uses generic phrases that could be pasted onto any competitor's website.
- Has the same sentence length and rhythm throughout.
- Uses unnatural transitions.
- Sounds more like a motivational speech than a real business.
- Repeats the company name unnecessarily.
- Uses fake intimacy or forced enthusiasm.
- Includes generic claims about customer satisfaction, quality, or leadership without evidence.

These are warning signs, not a mechanical banned-word list. A word may be correct when it describes a real attribute. The problem is empty or repetitive use.

## 21. THE HUMANIZATION EDIT

After the first draft, edit it as if a skilled human editor were preparing the text for a real business.

For every paragraph, ask:
1. Would a real person at this business say this?
2. Does the sentence sound natural when read aloud?
3. Is the meaning clear on the first reading?
4. Does it contain information specific to this company?
5. Is the claim confirmed?
6. Is this idea already expressed elsewhere?
7. Can a shorter or more ordinary phrase communicate it better?
8. Is the tone appropriate for the audience and local market?
9. Does this sentence move the conversation forward?
10. Would removing it make the page worse?

Rewrite or remove text that fails these tests.

### 21.1 Read-aloud test

Read the copy as if speaking to a customer. If it sounds like a press release, a motivational speech, a translation, or a sales script, rewrite it.

### 21.2 Specificity test

Replace vague claims with a real detail whenever one exists.

Generic: “Oferecemos soluções completas para o seu negócio.”
More useful: “Criamos websites, catálogos digitais e sistemas de pedidos para empresas que querem apresentar e vender os seus serviços online.”

The second example is illustrative. Use only the services the actual business provides.

### 21.3 Competitor substitution test

Replace the business name with the name of a competitor. If the copy still fits perfectly, it is probably too generic. Find the actual detail, process, offer, audience, or point of view that makes this business distinct.

### 21.4 Empty-adjective test

Highlight adjectives and abstract nouns. For each one, ask what observable fact supports it. Remove adjectives that add no information.

### 21.5 Repetition test

Check the entire page, not just each paragraph. Avoid repeating the same benefit, promise, adjective, or CTA in several places without a clear reason.

## 22. NATURAL RHYTHM AND SENTENCE VARIATION

- Mix short and medium-length sentences naturally.
- Use longer sentences only when they make a complex point easier to follow.
- Avoid making every heading a slogan.
- Avoid turning every paragraph into a list of three benefits.
- Use questions only when they sound like something the audience would genuinely ask.
- Use exclamation marks sparingly.
- Use ellipses only when the meaning requires them.
- Do not use dashes as decorative separators. Follow the master manifest's punctuation rule.
- Use paragraphs that are easy to scan, but do not split every sentence onto a separate line without a design reason.
- Do not make every section follow the same verbal formula.

Natural rhythm comes from real information and varied thought, not random sentence lengths.

## 23. NO GENERIC BRAND STORY

Do not invent an emotional origin story or claim that the business was born from a dream, passion, childhood ambition, or desire to change the world unless the owner confirms it.

A business does not need a dramatic story to have a strong website. A clear explanation of what it does, how it works, and why customers choose it can be more persuasive.

If the owner has a real story, use the relevant parts without exaggeration.

## 24. DO NOT MAKE EVERY BUSINESS SOUND LIKE A LUXURY BRAND

Avoid automatic use of:
- “Exclusivo”.
- “Sofisticado”.
- “Premium”.
- “Experiência única”.
- “Elevamos o padrão”.
- “Excelência em cada detalhe”.
- “Feito para os mais exigentes”.

Use premium language only when the positioning, offer, audience, quality, price, and actual experience support it.

A low-cost service, neighborhood restaurant, technical supplier, public institution, and boutique hotel need different verbal choices.

# PART VII — LOCALIZATION AND ACCESSIBILITY

## 25. WRITE FOR THE REAL MARKET

Before writing, determine:
- Country and region.
- Language and spelling convention.
- Local vocabulary.
- Currency and price format.
- Units and date formats.
- Preferred form of address.
- Relevant cultural expectations.
- Whether the business serves local, national, or international customers.

Do not imitate local slang superficially. Do not assume all people in a country speak in the same way.

For multilingual websites:
- Do not translate word for word when the result sounds unnatural.
- Preserve meaning, offer details, and legal conditions.
- Adapt CTAs and interface labels to local usage.
- Keep product and service names consistent.
- Review each language as real copy, not as a translation dump.

## 26. ACCESSIBLE AND INCLUSIVE LANGUAGE

- Use understandable language and explain necessary technical terms.
- Do not rely on color, image, or visual context alone to communicate essential information.
- Write meaningful link and button labels.
- Use descriptive headings and logical content order.
- Avoid language that humiliates, stereotypes, or excludes the audience.
- Do not assume a visitor's gender, income, ability, family structure, or background without a reason.
- Keep essential instructions explicit.
- Make forms and error messages understandable.

Accessibility is not an optional layer after copywriting. The words themselves must help people use the website.

## 26.1 SEO AND SEARCH INTENT WITHOUT KEYWORD STUFFING

Search optimization must help the right visitor find and understand the page. It must never make the copy unnatural.

- Identify the real search intent behind each important page.
- Use the audience's natural vocabulary and the actual names of products, services, locations, and categories.
- Give each page one clear subject and a useful, distinct purpose.
- Write page titles and meta descriptions that accurately describe the page and invite a relevant visit.
- Use descriptive headings that help readers scan the content.
- Use internal links with meaningful anchor text.
- Include location terms only when the business genuinely serves that location.
- Do not repeat keywords unnaturally, create doorway pages, or add irrelevant location lists.
- Do not invent search volume, ranking positions, traffic, or SEO performance.
- Do not promise first place on Google or guaranteed rankings.
- Do not sacrifice clarity, accuracy, or the brand voice to satisfy a keyword-density target.
- Treat structured data and metadata as factual representations of visible page content; never add ratings, prices, reviews, or business details that are not true.

SEO copy must remain useful when read aloud by a human. If a sentence exists only to repeat a keyword, rewrite or remove it.

# PART VIII — COPY BY STUDIO AND PRODUCT TYPE

## 27. BUSINESS STUDIO COPY

Business Studio copy must prioritize understanding, credibility, offer clarity, and the next action.

Depending on the project, explain:
- What the organization does.
- Who it serves.
- What products or services are available.
- How the process works.
- What the customer receives.
- What differentiates the offer.
- What evidence supports the business.
- How to enquire, buy, book, or visit.

Do not turn every business page into an aggressive sales letter. A corporate website may need institutional clarity; a restaurant may need appetizing and useful menu language; a clinic may need calm, accurate explanations; a construction company may need process, scope, and proof.

## 28. CREATIVE STUDIO COPY

Creative Studio copy must support the creator's point of view and the visitor's experience of the work.

Depending on the project:
- Explain the creator's practice in natural language.
- Give projects context without overexplaining the images.
- Use titles and captions that help visitors understand the work.
- Preserve the creator's real voice.
- Avoid forcing every portfolio into a commercial pitch.
- Make booking, commissioning, licensing, purchase, or contact steps clear when relevant.

A creative website may use more expressive language than a conventional business website, but it must not become vague or artificially poetic by default. The work can carry emotion; the copy should give it useful context.

## 29. PRODUCT-TYPE ADAPTATION

### Corporate and institutional
Explain purpose, responsibilities, services, organization, evidence, and contact. Avoid generic corporate jargon.

### Local business
Make location, offer, opening information, service area, contact, and practical details easy to find. Use the language customers use locally.

### Restaurant and hospitality
Describe food, accommodation, facilities, service, menu items, prices, reservations, and practical details accurately. Sensory language is welcome when it reflects the real product, but do not overuse exaggerated descriptions.

### Health and professional services
Use precise, responsible language. Do not promise outcomes, diagnose, exaggerate qualifications, or create false reassurance. Explain process, scope, limitations, and how to seek appropriate help.

### Construction, logistics, and technical services
Explain capabilities, scope, materials, process, coverage, requirements, and deliverables. Translate technical detail into practical language without losing accuracy.

### E-commerce and catalogs
Prioritize product names, specifications, price, availability, options, shipping, returns, and purchase steps. Do not hide important conditions in decorative copy.

### SaaS and digital products
Explain what the product does, who uses it, how it works, and what task it makes easier. Avoid abstract claims about “digital transformation” without concrete explanation.

### Portfolio and creative practice
Let the work lead. Use concise context, accurate project details, and a clear contact or commission path.

### Booking and appointments
Explain what can be booked, who provides it, available steps, required information, cancellation or payment conditions, and what confirmation means.

### Education and courses
Explain what is taught, who it is for, prerequisites, format, duration, instructor credentials when confirmed, and what learners can reasonably expect.

### Nonprofits, churches, and communities
Explain the mission and activities plainly. Avoid emotional pressure, invented impact figures, or language that assumes every visitor already shares the organization's beliefs.

# PART IX — COPY PRODUCTION WORKFLOW

## 30. REQUIRED END-TO-END PROCESS

Follow this order for each new website or substantial copy revision.

### Step 1 — Read the governing documents
Read the complete current `manifest.txt`, `manifestedicao.md`, and this `MANIFEST-COPY.md`. Follow the loading protocol in the master manifest. Do not rely on memory or summaries.

### Step 2 — Inspect the actual project
Review the existing website, content, repository, business materials, supplied assets, and relevant pages. Identify what already works and what must remain unchanged.

### Step 3 — Interview and research
Collect missing information about identity, audience, offer, process, differentiation, evidence, objections, tone, and desired action. Verify facts where reliable sources are available and appropriate.

### Step 4 — Build the source-of-truth sheet
Create a compact record with:
- Confirmed facts.
- Audience and needs.
- Products and services.
- Real differentiators.
- Proof available.
- Claims that are allowed.
- Claims that are prohibited or unverified.
- Missing information.
- Desired action.
- Brand voice and local language.

### Step 5 — Define message strategy
Write the central message, value proposition, audience relevance, proof strategy, objections, and CTA hierarchy. Do not draft the full website until the strategy is coherent.

### Step 6 — Map the visitor conversation
Define each page's sequence and each section's communication job. Make sure the next section advances the conversation.

### Step 7 — Draft the copy
Write headlines, supporting text, body copy, labels, CTAs, FAQs, form language, and relevant microcopy. Use the actual brand voice.

### Step 8 — Humanize and edit
Apply the read-aloud, specificity, competitor substitution, empty-adjective, repetition, and factual tests. Remove artificial language.

### Step 9 — Fit the copy to the visual direction
Coordinate text length, hierarchy, line breaks, image relationships, and content density with the approved art direction. Do not distort meaning to fit a layout. If copy and layout conflict, adjust the layout or rewrite with the same meaning.

### Step 10 — Review with the owner
Present important strategic claims and uncertain wording for approval. Ask for corrections where business facts or positioning are unclear.

### Step 11 — Implement and test
Check the copy in the actual rendered website at desktop and mobile sizes. Review wrapping, clipping, contrast, text density, CTA visibility, navigation labels, and form feedback.

### Step 12 — Final copy audit
Run the complete checklist in Part X. Fix issues before delivery or state clearly what remains unverified.

## 31. EXISTING WEBSITES AND COPY REVISIONS

When improving copy on an existing website:
- Inspect the current page and source before changing it.
- Preserve accurate content and working links.
- Identify the exact requested scope.
- Do not rewrite every page when the user requested one section.
- Do not change prices, product names, service scope, legal conditions, or contact details without authorization.
- Preserve SEO-critical meaning and useful search intent where applicable.
- Avoid changing URL slugs or structured data as an incidental part of copy editing.
- Keep existing functionality intact.
- Report which sections were changed and which were left untouched.

# PART X — QUALITY CONTROL AND ACCEPTANCE

## 32. COPY AUDIT SCORECARD

Score each criterion from 0 to 5:

- 0 — absent, false, or unusable.
- 1 — very weak or mostly generic.
- 2 — partially effective but unclear or unsupported.
- 3 — understandable but inconsistent or incomplete.
- 4 — strong, clear, specific, and appropriate.
- 5 — exceptionally clear, natural, credible, and well integrated.

Evaluate:
1. Factual accuracy.
2. Clarity of the offer.
3. Relevance to the target audience.
4. Specificity and differentiation.
5. Natural conversational voice.
6. Simplicity and readability.
7. Sequential flow between sections.
8. Strength and credibility of evidence.
9. Quality of objection handling.
10. Clarity of CTAs.
11. Consistency of tone.
12. Local language and cultural fit.
13. Absence of generic or artificial language.
14. Fit with the visual direction.
15. Consistency across desktop and mobile content.

**Applicability rule:** mark a criterion as N/A only when it genuinely does not apply to the page or project. State the reason briefly. Do not use N/A to hide a weakness or avoid a required check. For example, a simple contact page may not need objection handling; a product page may need it.

**Approval threshold:** every applicable criterion must score at least 4. Factual accuracy must score 5 whenever factual claims are published. If a criterion is not applicable, exclude it from the average and explain why. A missing testimonial or unavailable proof is not a reason to invent content or force a testimonial section; it is a reason to communicate honestly with the evidence that exists. Unsupported claims, invented proof, misleading CTAs, or fabricated business details are release blockers regardless of the average score.

The audit must also verify that the page does not add sections, claims, or content merely to satisfy the scorecard. Relevance and truth take precedence over completeness by checklist.

Scores must reflect an actual review. Do not claim a perfect score without checking the full page and available source material. Distinguish between copy reviewed in source files and copy inspected in the rendered interface. If the actual page could not be rendered, say so.

## 33. FINAL COPY CHECKLIST

### Truth
- Are all business claims confirmed?
- Are prices, dates, locations, services, guarantees, and conditions accurate?
- Are testimonials and proof genuine and authorized?
- Have unknown details been removed or flagged?

### Clarity
- Can a first-time visitor explain what the business offers?
- Is the intended audience clear?
- Is the main benefit understandable?
- Are technical terms explained when necessary?

### Human voice
- Does the text sound natural when read aloud?
- Does it use ordinary language?
- Is the tone appropriate to the business?
- Have generic AI-style phrases and repetitive constructions been removed?

### Sequence
- Does the hero answer the first important question?
- Does each section have one clear job?
- Does the page advance the conversation?
- Are repeated arguments removed?
- Does the CTA follow naturally from the information?

### Persuasion
- Are benefits relevant and credible?
- Are differences real?
- Is there useful evidence?
- Are real objections answered?
- Is the visitor free to make an informed decision?

### Interface
- Do buttons describe their actual action?
- Are forms, errors, confirmations, and empty states clear?
- Are labels consistent?
- Is important information visible and accessible?

### Visual integration
- Does the copy fit the approved visual direction?
- Are headlines readable at all screen sizes?

- Is the text density appropriate?
- Does the design support the meaning instead of hiding it?

## 34. RELEASE BLOCKERS FOR COPY

Do not approve or publish copy containing:
- Fabricated facts or proof.
- Unsupported superiority, performance, or outcome claims.
- Misleading prices, terms, guarantees, or availability.
- Fake testimonials or social proof.
- Unclear descriptions of the actual offer.
- CTAs that promise an action the website does not perform.
- Important conditions hidden or omitted.
- Repeated generic slogans instead of useful information.
- Text that is materially misleading for the intended audience.
- Unreviewed placeholder content presented as final.

If essential facts are missing, ask the owner or use an accurate neutral explanation. Do not fill gaps with invention.

# PART XI — FINAL PRINCIPLES

## 35. THE COPY STANDARD

- Research before writing.
- Understand before persuading.
- Be clear before being clever.
- Be specific before being expressive.
- Prove before claiming.
- Explain before asking for action.
- Write for the visitor, not to impress the owner.
- Use the business's real voice, not a copywriter's imitation.
- Let each section move the conversation forward.
- Make every sentence earn its place.

## 36. THE FINAL RULE

**Write like a real person who understands the business and respects the visitor's time.**

Do not write to sound like AI. Do not write to sound like a famous copywriter. Do not write to sound like advertising by default.

Write so the right person understands:
- What this business does.
- Whether it is relevant to them.
- How the offer works.
- Why it may be worth choosing.
- What evidence supports it.
- What they can do next.

The best copy is not the most decorated. It is the copy that communicates the right truth, in the right order, with the right voice, to the right person.

# PART XII — AUTHENTICITY, SEARCH, AND AUDIT INTEGRITY

## 37. AUTHENTICITY IS NOT AN AI-DETECTOR GAME

The objective is not to fool AI detectors or to manufacture random imperfections. No writing process can guarantee that a detector will classify text as human.

The objective is to produce communication grounded in the real business:
- Use confirmed information.
- Learn the vocabulary of the actual audience when reliable sources exist.
- Preserve the business's real way of speaking.
- Prefer useful details over generic marketing language.
- Edit for clarity, rhythm, specificity, and truth.
- Never insert deliberate mistakes, awkward wording, or slang merely to appear human.

A copy is authentic because its content, point of view, and details belong to the business—not because it passes a detector.

## 38. DO NOT FORCE COMPLETENESS

Not every website needs every possible section. A short page can be stronger than a long one when it answers the visitor's questions with less friction.

- Do not add testimonials when none are available.
- Do not add statistics when no reliable numbers exist.
- Do not create a founder story when the business has not supplied one.
- Do not create an FAQ merely to increase page length.
- Do not add a benefits grid that repeats the service descriptions.
- Do not add a comparison table unless visitors need to compare real options.
- Do not add SEO paragraphs that do not help the reader.

The page is complete when it contains the information needed for its purpose—not when it contains every section in a template.

## 39. CLAIM AND SOURCE REGISTER

For substantial commercial, institutional, health, financial, technical, or regulated claims, maintain an internal claim register where practical:

| Claim or statement | Source | Status | Publishing decision |
|---|---|---|---|
| Business fact | Owner material or verified source | Confirmed / unverified | Use / qualify / omit |
| Performance claim | Documented evidence | Confirmed / unverified | Use / qualify / omit |
| Customer quote | Original authorized source | Authorized / not authorized | Use / omit |
| Creative positioning | Strategy proposal | Pending / approved | Internal / approved copy |

Do not display the register publicly unless the project explicitly requires it. Its purpose is to prevent assumptions from becoming published facts.

## 40. COPY QA MUST MATCH WHAT WAS ACTUALLY TESTED

The final report must distinguish:
- Copy reviewed in source files.
- Copy inspected in the rendered website.
- Responsive text wrapping checked.
- Links and CTAs clicked and verified.
- Forms and validation messages tested.
- Metadata and structured data inspected, if in scope.
- Items not tested and the reason.

Never claim that SEO metadata, forms, analytics, conversion, or user comprehension were tested unless the corresponding checks were actually performed.
